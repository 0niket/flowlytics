# The Goal Has a Method

> <sub>_How to make more money without making more scrap — Goldratt, Little, and the levers of the line._</sub>

We had arrived at an answer: the goal is to make money. It felt like solid ground until I tried to stand on it, because "make money" is a destination, not a route. So Kaka asked the obvious follow-up — *"Fine. So how do I make* more *of it?"* — and I realized the answer was useless until I could turn it into something he could *do* on Monday. This is the story of finding the method hidden inside the goal: that profit isn't one number to chase but a tension between three — the money coming in, the money trapped on the line, and the money bleeding out — and that you cannot pull any single lever (another wagon, more baskets, a faster hoist) without the other two pushing back. It's the story of how a dead simple law about a line at a coffee shop explained why adding baskets made things *worse*, why a line where every machine looks busy can still be losing, and why the real job isn't to speed everything up — it's to find the one thing that's actually holding the line back, wring it dry, and only then spend a rupee. The goal is to make money. This is the method for actually doing it.

---

"More money," I said back to him, buying time. "So — make more parts. Run the line faster, push more baskets through, keep every wagon busy every second. More out the door, more revenue." Kaka didn't say anything. He just slid a sheet across the desk: last quarter's numbers from the line he'd already pushed hardest. Throughput up eleven percent. And underneath it, in his neat engineer's hand, two more lines — rework up nineteen percent, rejects up six. He tapped the second number. "We made more parts," he said. "Did we make more money?" I looked at the sheet for a long time. Revenue had gone up. But every reworked part is a part you pay for twice, and every reject is a part you pay for and then throw away. Somewhere in that eleven percent was a quieter number going the wrong way. *Faster* had a price, and the price was hidden in the scrap bin. The obvious lever — push harder — wasn't obviously anything.

So I went back to the scrap-bin question and tried to make it precise. Three kinds of money move through Kaka's line, I realized, and only three. There's the money coming *in* — but not when a part is made, only when a good part is *sold*; a part sitting in the scrap bin was never income, it was a costume that revenue wore for a few minutes before the rework log undressed it. There's the money going *out* every hour the line is open whether it ships anything or not — the operators, the power, the chemistry slowly exhausting in the tanks, the slow burn of machines wearing down. And there's a third, sneakier pile: the money *trapped* — frozen inside every basket currently on the line, parts you've already paid for but haven't been paid *for*, capital standing in a tank doing nothing but wait. Money in, money out, money stuck. And profit, I finally saw, wasn't any one of these — it was the *gap* between the first and the second, dragged on by the third. You don't maximize profit by maximizing the money coming in. You maximize it by widening a gap while a weight pulls against you.

But which lever? That's where I'd been stuck the night before — there were so many. Add a wagon. Add baskets. Shorten a dwell. Speed up the hoist. Move the tanks closer. Each one cost something; each one might help; I had no way to rank them. Then I remembered something Kaka had said on the floor, almost in passing, watching a basket sit in tank two while the wagon was away at the far end: *"Wagon is busy. Wagon is somewhere else."* The money coming in is just the rate good parts come *out*. And nothing comes out faster than the slowest hand in the relay can pass it along. On Kaka's line that hand was usually the wagon — one cart, thirteen moves per basket, and only so many seconds in an hour. It didn't matter how tight the dwell window was, or how cheap the baskets were, or how many I crammed onto the line. If the wagon couldn't keep up, *the wagon* set the pace of the money — and an hour the wagon lost wasn't an hour lost at the wagon. It was an hour lost by the whole line. Every lever that didn't make the wagon's hour count was, at best, rearranging furniture.

That last part nagged at me — *how many I crammed onto the line.* My instinct all along had been: more baskets, more parts. But Kaka's sheet said otherwise, and I couldn't see why. I saw it, finally, in a coffee shop. One barista, a line out the door. I counted: about twenty people waiting, and the barista finishing one cup roughly every thirty seconds. So the person who just walked in would wait about ten minutes — twenty cups times thirty seconds. Then a thought: if the manager wanted to *halve* that wait, what would work? Not letting more people into the queue — that makes the line longer, the wait worse. Only two things help: serve faster, or shorten the queue. The number waiting, the speed of serving, and the time each person waits aren't three independent dials. They're locked together: the crowd in the shop equals how fast you serve times how long each person stays. And the line on Kaka's floor is the same shop. The baskets on the line are the crowd. The rate good parts come out is the serving speed. The time a basket spends from load to done is the wait. Cram in more baskets without making the wagon serve faster, and you haven't added throughput — you've just made the line longer and every basket wait longer. And on this line a waiting basket isn't bored. It's sitting in a tank past its dwell window, turning into scrap.

Once I saw the line as that coffee shop, the law stopped being a warning and became a ruler. If I knew how fast the wagon could actually serve — the real rate, not the fantasy rate — and I knew how long a basket needed to spend from load to done, then I could compute the right number of baskets to have on the line at once instead of guessing it. You multiply the serving rate by the time in the system, and the result is the exact crowd the shop can hold before a queue starts to form. Keep fewer baskets than that and the wagon sits idle with nothing to serve, so you pay for an open line that earns less than it could. Push more baskets than that and they pile up waiting, over-dwelling, scrapping; the line looks fuller and busier while it quietly makes less. That was the trap in Kaka's quarter. He had read "everything is busy" as "everything is working," but a basket waiting in a tank is busy the way a man stuck in traffic is busy. A wagon running flat out only helps if it is running flat out serving, not chasing a backlog that should never have been let onto the floor. Busy is not the goal, and a full line is not the goal. The right-sized line carries exactly as many baskets as the slowest hand can keep moving, and that is the line that makes the most money with the least capital trapped inside it.

So I had the pieces, but Kaka's question had been *what do I do Monday*, and a set of pieces is not a procedure. I went back to him with what I'd worked out, and he listened, and then he did the thing he always does — he turned my insight into a sequence of steps a person could actually follow. "First," he said, "find the slow hand. Not where you think it is. Where it actually is, on the numbers." That was the wagon, usually, but not always; shorten enough dwells and the slow hand might become loading, or a single overworked tank. "Then," he went on, "before you spend one rupee, get everything you can out of that slow hand for free. Is it ever waiting on something it shouldn't? Is it carrying baskets that are already scrap? Make sure every move it makes is a move that counts." Only after that, he said, do you make the rest of the line *serve* the slow hand instead of running flat out for its own sake — which is just the right-sized-line rule, the wagon's pace setting everyone else's. And only after *that*, when you've wrung the slow hand completely dry and it's still the thing holding you back, do you finally spend money to make it faster or buy a second one. Then he smiled, because he knew what came next. "And when you do that, the slow hand moves somewhere else. So you start again." A procedure that eats its own tail. Find the constraint, exploit it, subordinate the line to it, elevate it, and the moment you've beaten it, go find the new one.

---

I walked the line one more time before I left, and it looked different. Not because anything had moved, but because I finally knew what I was looking at. The baskets weren't just parts in progress; they were capital, frozen, and there were either too many or too few. The wagon wasn't just a cart; it was the pace of the money, and every second it spent chasing a backlog was a second stolen from the whole line. A basket sitting a few seconds too long in a tank wasn't a small quality slip; it was revenue quietly converting itself into scrap. We had already settled the hard question — the goal is to make money — and I had walked away from that conversation thinking the hard part was behind me. It wasn't. Knowing the goal told me nothing about how to reach it, and the *how* turned out to be narrower than I expected and far more demanding. You make the most money by running the line at its right size, with no more baskets than the slowest hand can keep moving, so that not a single one over-dwells into scrap. Then you relentlessly find that slowest hand, wring it dry before you spend a rupee, and start over the moment it moves. The goal is to make money. The method is to maximize good throughput at zero violations by finding the constraint and elevating it, again and again. That is the whole job, and now it is something Kaka can do on Monday.
